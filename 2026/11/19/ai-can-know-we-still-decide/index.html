AI Can Know. We Still Decide.

Every leader building with AI faces the same question: what should humans do, and what should AI do? At the Haas Alumni Gathering in Tokyo, Dr. Homa Bahrami offered a clear answer: hand AI the knowledge work, keep the relationships, and own the decisions.

November 19, 2026 · 5 min read

There is one question that comes up in almost every leadership conversation I’m part of: what should humans do, and what should AI do?

I spend my days helping our organization become AI-native, so for me this question is practical, not philosophical. The answer shapes how we design workflows, how we structure teams and how we lead. Our leadership team comes back to it often, and because AI gets more capable every month, the answer never stays still for long.

So I was glad to hear someone take it on directly at the Haas Alumni Gathering in Tokyo on November 18.

More than 60 alumni came together that evening, the largest Haas alumni gathering outside the US. AI was on everyone’s mind. The main speaker was Dr. Homa Bahrami, Senior Lecturer at UC Berkeley’s Haas School of Business, whose work focuses on how organizations stay flexible in fast-moving markets. She co-wrote Super-Flexibility for Knowledge Enterprises with Stuart Evans.

Fittingly, she didn’t talk about models or tools. She started with people.

What humans essentially do

Her starting point was simple. Traditionally, human work has essentially come down to three things: we collect knowledge, we build relationships, and we make decisions. Careers have been built on doing all three well.

With AI in the picture, each of these changes in a different way.

Knowledge: hand it to AI. This is where AI is strongest. Gathering information, synthesizing it, finding patterns, recalling what was learned before. Work that once took years of experience or hours of research, AI can now do in seconds. This is where we should use it, and use it fully.

Relationships: keep them human. Trust, empathy, influence, the ability to bring people together. These remain human work, at least for now. AI can support relationships, but it can’t build them for us.

Decisions: use AI to think, not to decide. This is the part that is our choice, and where Dr. Bahrami was most deliberate. AI should be a counter-check, a source of alternatives, a way to provoke our thinking and question our assumptions. It can show us options we hadn’t considered and stress-test the ones we have. But the decision itself, and the accountability that comes with it, should stay with us.

What I value in this framing is that it answers the question most AI discussions skip. Not “where can AI be used?” but “where should it be used, and where shouldn’t it?”

When leaders stop knowing more

If AI takes over the knowledge work, something fundamental shifts for leaders.

Leadership, Dr. Bahrami pointed out, was built on an information gap. The leader knew more, and the relationship looked like parent and child. When everyone can ask the same AI, that gap closes. Authority that rests on “I know more” stops working.

So leaders need to move from parent–child to peer-to-peer relationships. She was specific about where this shows up: in every 1:1. Less instruction and status reporting, more thinking together.

Say it clearly, say it often

Her second point on leadership was just as direct: communicate clearly and frequently.

During AI-driven change, people need shared context to act with confidence. In a peer-to-peer organization, more decisions are made closer to the work, and people can only make good ones if they understand the why. Silence breeds uncertainty, and uncertainty about AI quickly turns into anxiety about roles.

Both of these are relationship work, the part she says stays human. That is the quiet irony of AI transformation: the more work AI takes on, the more the human side of leadership matters.

What this means for leaders building with AI

Her talk didn’t hand us a fixed answer, and with AI moving this fast, no one can. What it gives is a clear direction. Here is how I’m translating it into practice.

Design work by its type, not by the tool. When we look at a workflow, the first question isn’t “which model?” It’s “which part of this is knowledge work, which part is relationship work, and where is the decision?” Knowledge work is where AI should run hardest. Relationship work is where AI supports people. Decisions are where AI informs and humans own. Take coding: writing the code is largely knowledge work, but choosing the architecture is a decision, and agreeing on it is relationship work.

Make decision rights explicit. As AI grows more capable, the pull to let it decide grows too. Leaders should be deliberate about which decisions AI informs and who owns the outcome. Accountability can’t be delegated to a model.

Revisit the line regularly. What AI can do well today is different from six months ago. The boundary between human work and AI work is something a leadership team should keep revisiting, not settle once.

Lead as a peer. I can’t lead from “I know more” in a team where everyone has access to the same models. My role is to set context, build trust, ask good questions and communicate more, not less.

For our leadership team, this framing is a useful anchor for the conversations we keep having. Thought-provoking, and genuinely helpful in setting direction.

Students Always

Students Always is one of the Haas defining principles, and it was the theme of the evening. It felt especially fitting. With AI changing how we work and lead, staying a student isn’t optional anymore. It’s the job.

Thank you, Dr. Bahrami, the alumni panelists, Haas Alumni Relations, and everyone who made the evening happen. It was great reconnecting with fellow alumni.

Go Bears. 🐻
